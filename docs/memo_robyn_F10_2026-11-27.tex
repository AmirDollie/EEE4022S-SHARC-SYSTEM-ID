\head{2. Why a second class rule}
The thesis rule measures bias in Fisher units, $d=\sqrt{\delta\theta^\top F\,\delta\theta}$, roughly bias divided by $\sigma$. At p0 $\sigma_{\ln\beta}=0.05\%$, so physically negligible biases count as many $\sigma$ and fail the rule. Examples are $9\,\mu$m of sensor position, $0.008$\,mm of draught, or a fraction of a percent of wave energy from a second direction. The \emph{accuracy rule} classifies on the error in $\beta$ itself and keeps $d$ as a separate flag. One function implements both rules. The thesis rule stays the default, and both classes are reported side by side.

\begin{center}\small

\head{6. What this means for the paper}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Positioning, draught and model truncation are not the limiting errors.} Their real effect on $\beta$ is under 1\% for $\pm0.44$\,mm of sensor position and 12\% of draught. The thesis rule made them look fatal only because $\sigma$ is so small.
\item \textbf{Two sensors suffice when only accuracy is needed}, even with the heading unknown (best $e_\beta=0.35\%$). The thesis rule needed four.
\item \textbf{Directional seas are the genuine limitation.} No layout up to $n_s=4$ keeps $\beta$ within 5\% in every directional sea tested. Coherence, however, now warns in time.
\item \textbf{Stiff, small floes are limited by physics, not by the rule} ($R/\ell_f\lesssim0.6$).
\end{enumerate}
